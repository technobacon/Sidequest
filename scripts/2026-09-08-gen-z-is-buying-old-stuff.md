# Why Gen Z Is Buying Old Stuff

Draft: 8 September 2026. Approximately 1129 words. Target: roughly 8–9 minutes at a calm pace.

## Planning notes
Thesis: Older products can offer better value, a desired aesthetic, or useful limits; these motives overlap but do not demonstrate a wholesale rejection of modern technology.
Topic: Younger consumers' interest in used clothing, older cameras, iPods and physical music.
Stakes: Understanding what a purchase actually buys when a newer substitute is already available.
End goal: Explain the separate motives and why the old and new products coexist.
Intention: Get an affordable, enjoyable product suited to a specific activity.
Obstacle: Newness and feature count do not always satisfy that goal; revived demand can also undermine the old product's affordability.
Causal outline: Want affordable clothes BUT budget limits options → search used inventory THEREFORE resale becomes practical → BUT redundant cameras cannot be explained by savings alone → different aesthetics explain demand → BUT appearance does not explain distraction-free music players → limited functionality becomes useful → BUT inconvenience remains → physical ownership and collecting coexist with streaming → BUT discontinued supply cannot easily expand → judge specific utility rather than the vintage label.

Narration uses interpretation explicitly. No invented personal anecdotes. Industry-wide sales are not attributed to Gen Z. Forecasts are omitted.

## Narration

People are buying old digital cameras to take pictures, then moving those pictures onto the phones they could have taken them with.

Which sounds like a completely unnecessary extra step.

But apparently, that extra step is worth something.

Old cameras, iPods, secondhand clothes, vinyl. There's a whole market for things we were supposedly finished with, and younger buyers are part of what's keeping it going.

I wanted to understand why. Because if you're spending money on a device your phone already replaced, there has to be something you're getting back. And the answer changes quite a bit depending on what you're buying.

Start with clothes, because this is where the explanation is pretty straightforward.

You want something you like. Ideally something decent. But you also have a budget, so you check what it costs used.

In eBay's 2025 recommerce survey, 81% of respondents named saving money as a reason for buying pre-owned. That was well ahead of environmental benefits, at 45%.

That's across the surveyed consumers, by the way. It isn't a Gen Z-only number.

But it does put the whole thing in perspective. Before we turn buying a secondhand jacket into a statement about the future of society, sometimes the jacket is just cheaper.

And this is a substantial market. ThredUp's 2026 report, produced with GlobalData, says the U.S. secondhand clothing market grew nearly four times faster than the broader clothing market in 2025.

Now, ThredUp sells secondhand clothes, so obviously they're enthusiastic about that. But the distinction matters: people buying used isn't just a few vintage outfits appearing on your feed.

The buying process has changed, too.

If you want a particular jacket, you can search for the brand, size and color. You don't necessarily have to spend a Saturday hoping the right thing turns up in a local shop.

So buying old stuff can be a very normal way of getting something you want for less.

But then you get to cameras, and that explanation starts to break down.

Because the person buying one often already has a perfectly usable camera. It's in their pocket.

ABC interviewed young digital-camera users in 2025, including a woman in Perth who also sells them secondhand. She described people taking them on nights out specifically for the different look: the strong flash and the way the pictures come out.

They're looking for a particular result.

Think of the difference between a clean phone portrait and a photo where your friend is brightly lit, the room behind them has practically disappeared, and everyone's slightly caught off guard.

You might prefer the second one.

The older camera can be worse at producing a technically clean image and still be better at producing the picture you actually wanted.

And once you start from that intention, buying the camera makes more sense. You're choosing a look, much like choosing clothes.

You don't even have to remember using it the first time around. Someone can like the look of early-2000s party photos without having attended a party in the early 2000s.

But appearance isn't the whole appeal of carrying a separate device.

This is where the iPod gets interesting.

Apple wound down the last iPod in 2022. Its announcement pointed people toward listening on the iPhone, Apple Watch and its other devices. Which was a reasonable explanation. The music player had been absorbed into things people already owned.

And yet people are looking for them again.

Axios reported eBay searches for iPod Classics rose 25% in January–October 2025 versus the same period in 2024.

That's searches, not sales. Still, it's a funny afterlife for a discontinued music player.

The attraction some younger users describe is being able to listen without the other things that come with opening their phone. Euronews reported that motivation in its coverage of retro tech this August.

And I can see the appeal.

You want to change the song. On your phone, that also puts your messages and every other app within reach. On an iPod Classic, you change the song.

You're accepting the hassle of another device because it has a fairly useful limitation.

But it is still a hassle.

You need music files, you need to transfer them, and you might be dealing with an old battery. Finding a song somebody just recommended takes more effort than searching a streaming app.

So whether that's a good trade depends on what was annoying you in the first place. If you love instant access to everything, an old iPod could get frustrating very quickly.

Vinyl takes that trade even further.

The RIAA recorded about 46.8 million vinyl albums sold in the U.S. in 2025. And before we call that a Gen Z takeover, those figures include buyers of all ages. They're also sales of the format, including new releases, rather than just people buying old records.

Still, that's a lot of people buying a physical version of music they could often stream.

My read is that a record offers something different from access to the song. There's the artwork, the object you can collect or give someone, and the experience of putting an album on.

I wouldn't try to justify every record purchase by saying it's the most convenient way to listen. Obviously it isn't.

But buying a favorite album and paying for access to millions of songs can serve different purposes.

And that helps explain why these older formats can grow without replacing the new ones.

The same RIAA report puts streaming at roughly 82% of U.S. recorded-music revenue. Records are selling, and streaming is still overwhelmingly dominant.

You can have both.

But there's one problem with old stuff becoming desirable again: desirable stuff doesn't necessarily stay cheap.

If everyone starts looking for the same discontinued camera, more demand doesn't make another factory batch appear.

There are only so many working ones available. Sellers can see what people are looking for, and the bargain you heard about can become a collectible with a very different price.

That's the point where I'd separate wanting the thing from wanting to participate in the trend.

A secondhand jacket that saves you money, a camera whose photos you prefer, and a music player that helps you leave your phone alone all have understandable uses.

But the word “vintage” doesn't tell you whether something is a good purchase.

So I think “Gen Z is buying old stuff” bundles together several pretty ordinary decisions.

Some people want a lower price. Some want a particular look. Some want to do one thing without picking up the device that does everything.

The older product happens to fit.

And honestly, that seems like enough of an explanation. You can like your smartphone and still want the little camera. You just want to know what you're getting from carrying both.
