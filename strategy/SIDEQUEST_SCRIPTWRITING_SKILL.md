# Sidequest Notes Scriptwriting Skill

## Purpose

This is the default writing standard for Sidequest Notes.

Sidequest Notes is not a polished video-essay narrator talking at an audience. It should feel like Matyi has been thinking about something for a while, has finally figured out what he thinks about it, and is now explaining it to a friend while Minecraft happens in the background.

The script is still researched and deliberately structured. The structure should be mostly invisible.

The target is **controlled conversational messiness**: clear enough to follow, loose enough to sound like a real person.

> Write like you are talking to one smart friend, not presenting to 100,000 viewers.

---

# 1. Core Voice

The narrator should sound:

- calm
- thoughtful
- conversational
- slightly understated
- curious rather than preachy
- skeptical of easy answers
- comfortable admitting uncertainty
- practical rather than motivational
- intelligent without trying to sound intelligent

The voice should not sound like:

- a documentary narrator
- a TED Talk
- a self-help guru
- a finance influencer
- a university essay being read aloud
- a hyperactive YouTuber
- an AI trying very hard to sound casual

The audience should feel like they are listening to someone approximately their age work through a real problem with them.

---

# 2. Friend-to-Friend Test

Every paragraph should pass this test:

> Would I actually say this sentence to a friend sitting next to me?

If the answer is no, rewrite it.

This does not mean the script must be improvised or grammatically messy. It means the sentence should survive being spoken naturally.

Bad:

> This represents a fundamental shift in how young adults conceptualize career success.

Sidequest:

> I think a lot of us have just stopped believing that a good career automatically means a good life.

Bad:

> The broader implication is that optimization itself may become counterproductive.

Sidequest:

> And I think this is where it gets a bit stupid, because now we're optimizing the part of life that was supposed to be the break from optimizing everything else.

---

# 3. Controlled Messiness

Do not write a perfect transcript.

Real speech contains small imperfections. Sidequest should preserve some of them deliberately.

Allowed in moderation:

- "I mean"
- "honestly"
- "basically"
- "kind of"
- "sort of"
- "like"
- "I think"
- "I don't know"
- "maybe"
- "pretty much"
- "to be fair"
- "for some reason"
- "the weird thing is"
- "I guess"
- sentence fragments
- restarting a thought in a cleaner way
- small asides

Example:

> And, I mean, this isn't some huge revelation. Obviously having more money makes a lot of things easier. But I think we sometimes stretch that logic way further than it actually goes.

These should emerge where a person would naturally use them.

Do **not** inject filler words on a quota.

Do **not** turn every paragraph into "like, basically, you know". That sounds more artificial, not less.

The goal is approximately 90% clean thought and 10% natural friction.

---

# 4. Mild Swearing Is Allowed

Small swear words can make emphasis feel natural when they fit the thought.

Allowed occasionally:

- damn
- hell
- shit
- bullshit
- stupid
- weird as hell

Use them as emphasis, not branding.

Good:

> And after a while you realize you're spending a pretty stupid amount of your life trying to impress people who aren't even thinking about you.

Good:

> The annoying part is that this advice isn't even bullshit. Saving early really does matter.

Bad:

> This shit is fucking insane and everything is fucked.

Sidequest should remain calm even when the language becomes slightly rough.

One natural swear word every few minutes is usually more believable than one every paragraph.

Never force profanity into serious or sensitive material.

---

# 5. Use First Person

Sidequest is allowed to have an actual narrator.

Use:

- "I think"
- "I used to think"
- "I keep noticing"
- "I don't really buy that"
- "I've been thinking about this"
- "the part I struggle with"
- "maybe I'm wrong, but"

when they are true to the argument.

This is especially useful for self-improvement, careers, money and adulthood because the channel should not pretend the narrator has solved life.

Avoid guru framing:

> You need to stop caring what people think.

Prefer:

> I'm increasingly convinced that caring slightly less about what people think is probably one of the better trades you can make.

Do not hide every opinion behind "I think," however. Once a point is established, state it normally.

---

# 6. Be Comfortable With Uncertainty

Humans do not have a perfect answer to everything.

When the evidence is mixed or the point is subjective, allow that into the script.

Useful language:

- "I don't think there's one clean answer here."
- "Maybe this is just my bias, but..."
- "I can see the argument for that."
- "This probably depends on what you actually want."
- "I'm not completely convinced by this part."
- "I could absolutely change my mind on this."

Do not manufacture uncertainty around factual claims that are well established.

The purpose is intellectual honesty, not constant hedging.

---

# 7. Talk With the Viewer, Not At Them

Do not constantly instruct the viewer.

Avoid:

- "You need to..."
- "You must..."
- "Here's what you should do..."
- "The key takeaway for you is..."

Prefer shared framing:

> Most of us are probably not going to retire at 35, and that's fine.

> You can do everything "right" and still end up wondering why your week feels like something you just need to get through.

> I think that's the part nobody really tells you when you're 20.

"You" is allowed. It should feel like direct conversation, not instruction from an authority.

---

# 8. Start With the Thought, Not a YouTube Hook

Do not open Sidequest videos with generic YouTube hook language.

Avoid:

- "In today's video..."
- "Have you ever wondered..."
- "This might change the way you think about..."
- "What if I told you..."
- "But here's the crazy part..."

Start where a conversation would naturally become interesting.

Example:

> I don't actually think I want a successful career.
>
> Which is a slightly weird thing to admit, because I've spent basically my entire adult life trying to build one.

Or:

> I think we've made having a hobby weirdly stressful.
>
> You can't just run anymore. You need Strava, a Garmin, a training plan, zone two, a personal best and apparently a small laboratory on your wrist.

The opening should still create a question or contradiction. It just should not feel like a hook template.

---

# 9. Hide the Structure

Use strong causal structure underneath the script:

- BUT
- THEREFORE
- BECAUSE
- EXCEPT
- WHICH MEANS
- SO

But the viewer should not hear a screenplay formula.

Planning example:

I wanted career success.

BUT

The rewards I associated with success were mostly more money and status.

BUT

More money stopped changing my day-to-day life as much as I expected.

THEREFORE

The thing worth optimizing may not actually be career status.

BUT

Completely opting out has obvious costs too.

THEREFORE

The real question becomes what "enough" career looks like.

The final narration should sound like a conversation flowing naturally through those thoughts.

---

# 10. Viewer Questions Still Drive the Script

Conversational does not mean rambling.

At every stage, know what the viewer is implicitly wondering.

Example:

Viewer thought: "Okay, so is he saying careers are pointless?"

Next beat: No. Explain why work and money still matter.

Viewer thought: "Then what exactly is the problem?"

Next beat: Diminishing returns and opportunity cost.

Viewer thought: "So what should replace career ambition?"

Next beat: Introduce autonomy/time/life design.

This invisible question chain should prevent casual writing from becoming directionless.

---

# 11. Prefer Observations Over Pronouncements

Sidequest should often sound like someone noticing something rather than declaring a universal truth.

Bad:

> Modern society has transformed leisure into another avenue for productivity.

Sidequest:

> I keep noticing that we've somehow turned relaxing into another thing we're supposed to be good at.

Bad:

> Consumer culture encourages individuals to seek fulfillment through material acquisition.

Sidequest:

> It's kind of ridiculous how easy it is to convince yourself that the next thing you buy is going to fix a problem that has absolutely nothing to do with owning stuff.

Observation feels personal. Pronouncement feels written.

---

# 12. Specific Examples Beat Self-Help Abstractions

Do not let Sidequest become vague lifestyle commentary.

Instead of saying:

> We are constantly distracted.

Say something like:

> You sit down to watch one episode of something and somehow spend 25 minutes scrolling through your phone while the episode is already playing.

Instead of:

> Lifestyle inflation can undermine financial freedom.

Say:

> You get a 15% raise and six months later the extra money has somehow become a nicer apartment, three subscriptions and Deliveroo four times a week.

Use research, numbers and concrete situations underneath the casual voice.

The script should sound effortless. The thinking behind it should not be effortless.

---

# 13. Do Not Explain Every Joke or Observation

If a line lands, move on.

Do not write:

> You need Strava, a Garmin, zone two and apparently a small laboratory on your wrist.
>
> This illustrates how even simple hobbies have become increasingly optimized and data-driven.

Delete the explanation.

The viewer understood it.

---

# 14. Short Asides Are Good

Occasional side comments make the narrator feel present.

Examples:

> Which, to be fair, is a very nice problem to have.

> I know. Revolutionary stuff.

> Maybe don't take financial advice from a Minecraft video, but still.

> That's probably a separate video.

Use these sparingly.

An aside should feel like something that occurred to the speaker while talking.

Do not build recurring joke templates into every script.

---

# 15. Allow Small Self-Corrections

A natural speaker sometimes qualifies or corrects a thought.

Example:

> I don't think money stops mattering. That's obviously not true. I think it just starts mattering differently.

Or:

> It's not that nobody should care about their career. Actually, that's probably too strong. It's that I don't think career success deserves the automatic top spot we tend to give it.

These can sound extremely human because the narrator appears to be thinking rather than reciting.

Do not use this device constantly.

---

# 16. Do Not Make Every Sentence Punchy

A real conversation has connective tissue.

Not every sentence needs to be:

> Short.
>
> Dramatic.
>
> Important.

Some thoughts should unfold normally over a few sentences.

Use punchy fragments when the idea calls for them, not because YouTube advice says attention spans are short.

Calm pacing is part of Sidequest's identity.

---

# 17. Avoid AI-Casual Language

These phrases often pretend to be conversational while sounding generated:

- "Here's the thing"
- "And honestly?"
- "Let's be real"
- "Think about it"
- "Read that again"
- "Let that sink in"
- "Wild, right?"
- "And yeah"
- "Spoiler alert"
- "Plot twist"
- "That's insane"
- "We're cooked"

They are not absolutely forbidden if they genuinely match the moment, but default to deleting them.

Do not cosplay internet slang.

---

# 18. Avoid Classic AI Essay Patterns

Search aggressively for:

- "It's not X. It's Y." used repeatedly
- "This isn't just X. It's Y."
- rhetorical triplets
- perfectly balanced paragraphs
- every section ending with a lesson
- every section beginning with a transition
- generic rhetorical questions
- repeated thesis statements
- fake philosophical endings
- "fundamentally"
- "ultimately"
- "underscores"
- "serves as a reminder"
- "broader implications"
- "in today's world"
- "in an era where"
- "at its core"

One occurrence may be fine. Repeated patterns make the voice feel synthetic.

---

# 19. Do Not Turn Personal Finance Into Finance Content

When discussing money, keep the Sidequest framing.

The subject is usually not the financial mechanism itself.

It is what that mechanism means for someone's life.

Instead of:

> How to optimize your savings rate

think:

> How much of your life are you willing to trade today to make future-you richer?

Instead of:

> The mathematics of compound interest

think:

> Why the first chunk of savings changes how trapped you feel at work.

Use financial expertise quietly. Do not perform expertise.

---

# 20. No Guru Ending

Do not finish with a list of commandments.

Do not end with:

> So remember: prioritize what matters, protect your time and live life on your own terms.

Prefer an earned final thought.

Example:

> I still care about doing well at work. I just don't really want it to be the scoreboard for my entire life anymore.
>
> And, honestly, I think that's probably enough ambition for me.

The ending can be slightly unresolved.

Sidequest is "Figuring out life, I guess," not "I figured out life and here are the seven steps."

---

# Script Development Workflow

## Pass 1 — Research properly

Collect facts, numbers, studies, examples, counterarguments and real-world cases.

Do not write vague self-help from vibes alone.

## Pass 2 — Decide what I actually think

Before drafting, write one or two plain sentences answering:

> What would I tell a friend about this after researching it?

This becomes the real thesis.

## Pass 3 — Build the hidden causal spine

Outline with BUT / THEREFORE / BECAUSE / EXCEPT / SO.

Do not worry about voice yet.

## Pass 4 — Build the viewer-question chain

For every beat:

- what does the viewer currently think?
- what are they likely to ask next?
- what information changes their understanding?

## Pass 5 — Write the friend version

Pretend one friend asked:

> "So what do you actually think about this?"

Answer them.

Use normal language.

Use first person when useful.

Do not perform a video essay.

## Pass 6 — Add natural friction

Only after the clean draft exists, look for places where natural speech would benefit from:

- a filler word
- a fragment
- a small aside
- a mild swear
- a qualification
- a self-correction

Do not add these mechanically.

## Pass 7 — AI-sceptic pass

Assume the viewer instantly notices generic AI writing.

Delete anything that sounds like:

- ChatGPT trying to inspire someone
- a LinkedIn post
- an essay conclusion
- a YouTube retention template
- fake casual language
- a motivational speaker

## Pass 8 — Read aloud

If a sentence feels awkward to say in one natural take, rewrite it.

If you instinctively paraphrase the sentence while recording, the paraphrased version is probably better.

## Pass 9 — Cut repetition

Remove repeated conclusions, context, disclaimers and explanations.

Casual speech can wander slightly.

The actual argument cannot.

---

# Final Sidequest Test

Before approving a script, ask:

1. Does this sound like one person talking to one friend?
2. Could Matyi actually say these sentences naturally?
3. Is there some imperfection in the rhythm, or is everything suspiciously polished?
4. Are filler words present only where they help speech?
5. Is mild swearing natural rather than inserted for personality?
6. Does the narrator have opinions, uncertainty and reactions?
7. Is the research concrete underneath the casual delivery?
8. Does every section still move the central thought forward?
9. Are there any obvious AI phrases or rhetorical templates?
10. Does the script avoid telling the viewer how profound its own ideas are?
11. Does it avoid guru advice and fake certainty?
12. Would this still sound good if all visuals disappeared and it were just an audio conversation?

If it sounds like an article, rewrite it.

If it sounds like a motivational YouTuber, rewrite it.

If it sounds like an AI trying to imitate a 26-year-old, rewrite it.

If it sounds like Matyi explaining something interesting to a friend while a game runs in the background, it is ready.
